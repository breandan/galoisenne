The construction above gives us an exact finite language,
\(\ell_\cap=\mathcal{L}(G)\cap\mathcal{L}\big(L(\err\sigma,d)\big)\), whose elements are precisely the valid repairs within the allowed edit radius.
What remains is a ranking problem: among the strings that are already valid and nearby, which should be shown first?

\subsection{Intersection decoding}

We represent \(G_\cap\) as the star-free regular expression described in \S~\ref{sec:measurement}.
Given a partial repair prefix \(\sigma_{0\ldots i}\) and residual expression \(e_i\), the Brzozowski derivative exposes the next legal symbols:
\[
  \texttt{follow}(e_i)=\{a\in\Sigma \mid \mathcal{L}(\partial_a e_i)\neq\varnothing\}.
\]
A decoder therefore maintains a priority queue of triples \(\langle \sigma,e,s\rangle\), where \(e\) is the residual expression and \(s\) is a running log score.
Popping the highest-scoring prefix and pushing \(\langle\sigma a,\partial_a e,s+\log P(a\mid\sigma)\rangle\) for every legal \(a\) yields a best-first traversal of \(\ell_\cap\).
Whenever \(\varepsilon\in\mathcal{L}(e)\), the prefix is a complete repair.
Because every transition is guarded by the derivative, this procedure never proposes an invalid string and never wastes effort on rejection sampling.

The choice of \(P(a\mid\sigma)\) controls the retrieval order.
Our current implementation uses a smoothed \(c\)-gram model because it is small, cache-friendly and fast enough to saturate many intersections within an interactive latency budget.
However, the \(c\)-gram score is not essential to the construction.
Any left-to-right scoring function that can be queried at decoder speed may be substituted, including a weighted deterministic finite automaton trained as a regular overapproximation to the target programming language.

\subsection{Path-dependent retrieval distributions}

Several familiar decoding strategies are tempting at this point.
One can view \(G_\cap\) as a finite hypergraph and apply \(k\)-best dynamic programming or cube pruning~\cite{huang2005better,chiang2007hierarchical}.
One can also use non-autoregressive strategies, such as edit-based or diffusion-style denoisers, to propose repairs in parallel rather than committing to a left-to-right order~\cite{austin2021structured,havasi2025edit}.
We found these approaches mismatched to the role of the first-stage decoder.
The retriever must enumerate, or sample without replacement from, a finite language of unique strings; it must preserve completeness when the search is allowed to run to saturation; and, before saturation, its prefix order should approximate the distribution induced by a left-to-right language model over the same finite set.
Cube pruning is useful when the scoring model decomposes over grammar items, but in practice it gives an approximate frontier over derivations.
With ambiguous grammars, high-scoring derivations need not be high-scoring distinct strings, and duplicate strings must be merged to obtain sampling without replacement.
Non-autoregressive proposals have the complementary problem: they can explore many edit locations at once, but they do not expose a prefix probability \(P(a\mid\sigma)\) that can drive derivative decoding, and they require an additional de-duplication and validity mechanism to recover the exact finite CFL.

The target retrieval distribution is therefore not a distribution over parse trees or edit trajectories.
Let \(P_\theta\) be a left-to-right string model.
For a repair instance, the finite conditional distribution we would like to approximate is
\[
  P_\cap(\sigma)=
  \frac{P_\theta(\sigma)}
       {\sum_{\tau\in\ell_\cap}P_\theta(\tau)}
  \qquad\text{for }\sigma\in\ell_\cap.
\]
The normalizer is constant for a fixed instance, so ranking by \(P_\cap\) is equivalent to ranking by the left-to-right string probability \(P_\theta(\sigma)\), while the support remains exactly context-free.
This is the sense in which the distribution is structurally context-free but distributionally sequential: the grammar determines which strings exist, but the probability model should still be allowed to depend on the path taken through the string prefix.
Derivative decoding gives precisely this interface.
The residual expression \(e_i\) enforces the remaining CFL constraint, while the score attached to the prefix \(\sigma_{0\ldots i}\) can be supplied by any fast path-dependent model.

This distinction is why a PCFG would not be a drop-in replacement for our decoder.
A PCFG assigns probability to local production choices and induces a string probability only after summing over all derivations of that string.
After intersection with the Levenshtein automaton, exact ranking would require weighted inside computations over the residual grammar and careful merging of ambiguous derivations.
Top-down ancestral sampling from the PCFG samples derivations with replacement, not distinct strings from \(P_\cap\), and can place most of its mass on a small derivational mode even when many valid repairs remain hidden elsewhere in the finite language.
Thus a PCFG is structurally natural but distributionally inconvenient for our retrieval objective.
It gives the wrong primitive: grammar-local expansion probabilities, rather than a cheap left-to-right conditional \(P(a\mid\sigma)\) over the unique-string frontier.

The \(c\)-gram model used in our current implementation is the simplest such path-dependent scorer.
It is fast, deterministic, and compatible with duplicate-free enumeration, but its Markov assumption is too strong: all history beyond the last \(c-1\) symbols is discarded.
Program naturalness often depends on weak longer-range signals, such as delimiter context, indentation regime, recently introduced identifiers, or the fact that a repair prefix has already committed to one side of an ambiguous syntactic pattern.
A weighted finite automaton is a better compromise.
Its state is still finite and cheap to update during derivative decoding, but it can learn a coarser latent summary of the prefix than an explicit \(c\)-gram suffix.
The WFA is not responsible for recognizing the programming language; that remains the job of \(G_\cap\).
Instead, it supplies a weakly path-dependent approximation to the left-to-right distribution on top of an exact CFL support.

\subsection{Language-overapproximating WFDFA}

The weighted intersection construction of Pasti et al.~\cite{pasti2023intersection} is the natural comparison point: if one had a weighted CFG and a weighted Levenshtein automaton, their intersection would give a weighted grammar for repairs.
For syntax repair, however, the difficult weight assignment is exactly on the Levenshtein automaton.
The automaton \(L(\err\sigma,d)\) is built from the broken string, so its transition weights would be an instance-specific error model over edit histories.
Without a calibrated model of how the user produced \(\err\sigma\), those weights would be arbitrary; if they were then used to prune low-weight product rules, completeness would depend on an unprincipled, broken-string-dependent noise model.

We instead separate support from scoring.
This approach trains a weighted deterministic finite automaton (WFDFA), \(\alpha_{\hat\theta}\), as a fast surrogate for a larger language model \(P_\theta\).
Let \(\alpha\) be a Nederhof-style regular overapproximation of the grammar~\cite{nederhof2000practical,mohri2001regular}, whose Boolean support preserves the hard syntactic invariant
\[
  \mathcal{L}(G)\subseteq\mathcal{L}(\alpha).
\]
The weighted automaton \(\alpha_{\hat\theta}\) has the same support, positive transition weights on every retained edge, and proper final weights so that it induces a probability distribution \(P_{\hat\theta}\) over \(\mathcal{L}(\alpha)\).
Unlike the Levenshtein automaton, \(\alpha_{\hat\theta}\) is learned once from valid programs and is invariant to the broken string and the repair radius.
By conditioning this distribution on the true grammar, it induces a language prior
\[
  P_{\hat\theta}^{G}(\sigma)=
  \frac{P_{\hat\theta}(\sigma)}
       {\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{L}(G)}P_{\hat\theta}(\tau)}
  \qquad\text{for }\sigma\in\mathcal{L}(G),
\]
whose weights approximate \(P_\theta(\sigma\mid\sigma\in\mathcal{L}(G))\) while the Boolean support remains an overapproximation.
For a repair instance,
\[
  \ell_\cap=\mathcal{L}(G)\cap\mathcal{L}\big(L(\err\sigma,d)\big),
\]
we use \(P_{\hat\theta}\) only to order candidates that the exact intersection has already admitted.

\noindent\textbf{Claim.}
Every repair in \(\ell_\cap\) is recognized by the Nederhof overapproximation, and the WFDFA weights induce well-defined distributions on \(\mathcal{L}(G)\) and \(\ell_\cap\).

\noindent\emph{Proof.}
If \(\sigma\in\ell_\cap\), then by definition \(\sigma\in\mathcal{L}(G)\).
Since \(\mathcal{L}(G)\subseteq\mathcal{L}(\alpha)\), it follows immediately that \(\sigma\in\mathcal{L}(\alpha)\).
Thus no syntactically valid repair can be excluded by the overapproximation.
Because \(\alpha_{\hat\theta}\) is deterministic and proper, each \(\sigma\in\mathcal{L}(\alpha)\) receives a nonnegative string probability \(P_{\hat\theta}(\sigma)\), and every \(\sigma\in\ell_\cap\) receives positive mass under the positive-edge assumption.
Since \(\mathcal{L}(G)\subseteq\mathcal{L}(\alpha)\), conditioning \(P_{\hat\theta}\) on \(\mathcal{L}(G)\) gives \(P_{\hat\theta}^{G}\), whose normalizer is finite because \(P_{\hat\theta}\) is a probability distribution over \(\mathcal{L}(\alpha)\).
Conditioning further on the finite repair language gives
\[
  P_{\hat\theta}^{\cap}(\sigma)=
  \frac{P_{\hat\theta}(\sigma)}
       {\sum_{\tau\in\ell_\cap}P_{\hat\theta}(\tau)}
  \qquad\text{for }\sigma\in\ell_\cap,
\]
which is a distribution because the denominator is finite and positive whenever \(\ell_\cap\neq\varnothing\).
\(\square\)

The tradeoff is that \(P_{\hat\theta}^{\cap}\) is a language prior, not a complete conditional repair model.
It approximates the true distribution over valid programs by learning weights on an overapproximating regular language, while the exact CFL--Levenshtein intersection continues to enforce validity and edit distance.
This avoids excluding valid repairs and avoids assigning ad hoc weights to edit paths, but it leaves broken-string-specific preferences to the reranker.
The overapproximation may contain strings outside \(\mathcal{L}(G)\), but those strings are harmless when the exact grammar intersection remains the admissibility filter.

We measure the tightness of a finite-prefix overapproximation by comparing the number of strings admitted up to length \(m\):
\[
  \rho(\alpha,G,m)=
  \frac{\sum_{n=1}^m |\mathcal{L}(G)\cap\Sigma^n|}
       {\sum_{n=1}^m |\mathcal{L}(\alpha)\cap\Sigma^n|}.
\]
Higher \(\rho\) means less spurious regular language volume below the cutoff.
The training objective therefore has two parts: fit weights to the teacher distribution, and keep the support tight without ever losing inclusion.
We estimate the distributional term by samples from \(P_\theta\) and minimize
\[
  D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(P_\theta(\cdot\mid\mathcal{L}(G))\,\middle\|\,P_{\hat\theta}\right),
\]
or its minibatch cross-entropy approximation.
The structural term is enforced by counterexample-guided refinement rather than by a soft penalty.

Concretely, we initialize \(\alpha\) with Nederhof's \(d\)-RTN approximation: recursive calls in the grammar are unfolded for \(d\) rounds, and any remaining call edges are routed through shared nonterminal automata.
This produces a regular language that contains \(\mathcal{L}(G)\), with larger \(d\) typically giving a tighter but larger automaton.
We then alternate between weight fitting and support refinement:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Fit the outgoing transition weights of \(\alpha_{\hat\theta}\) against teacher samples by normalized path likelihood.
  \item Propose a local topology refinement that improves held-out likelihood and increases \(\rho(\alpha,G,m)\), subject to a state budget \(|Q|_{\max}\).
  \item Binarize the proposal and verify inclusion by checking whether \(\mathcal{L}(G)\cap\overline{\mathcal{L}(\alpha)}=\varnothing\).
  \item If inclusion fails, add witnesses from \(\mathcal{L}(G)\cap\overline{\mathcal{L}(\alpha)}\) back into the automaton, renormalize affected transitions, and continue.
\end{enumerate}

The result is not meant to replace the exact repair language.
Rather, it gives us a fast scoring automaton whose support is guaranteed to cover every syntactically valid program.
At decoding time we can use \(P_{\hat\theta}\) in place of the \(c\)-gram model in the priority queue, or use it as a proposal model before exact derivative filtering.
This is the main expected advantage over constrained LLM decoding: exhaustive ranking under a low-fidelity but high-throughput approximation can retrieve far more valid candidates than a highly calibrated model that only explores a narrow beam.

\subsection{Reranking}

The retriever is intentionally syntax-first and mostly context-free: it scores a repair candidate \(\sigma\) as a string, not as a fix for the particular broken input \(\err\sigma\).
The reranker supplies this missing conditional information.
For each training repair pair \(\langle\err\sigma,\sigma^\star\rangle\), we decode a confounder set \(\mathcal{D}_{\err\sigma}\subseteq\ell_\cap\) without replacement and train a Levenshtein-aligned transformer reranker to put \(\sigma^\star\) above the sampled alternatives.
Let \(E_\theta(\err\sigma)\) encode the broken snippet and \(E'_\theta(\sigma,\mathrm{LA}(\err\sigma,\sigma))\) encode a candidate together with its edit alignment.
The relevance score is
\[
  f_\theta(\err\sigma,\sigma)=
  \mathrm{MLP}_\theta\!\left(E_\theta(\err\sigma),E'_\theta(\sigma,\mathrm{LA}(\err\sigma,\sigma))\right).
\]
We train with a listwise cross-entropy loss over the retrieved list:
\[
  \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{rank}}(\theta)=
  -\sum_{\err\sigma}
  \log
  \frac{\exp(f_\theta(\err\sigma,\sigma^\star)/\tau)}
       {\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{D}_{\err\sigma}}\exp(f_\theta(\err\sigma,\sigma)/\tau)}.
\]
The final repair procedure is therefore retrieve-then-rerank:
construct \(\ell_\cap\), decode a large prefix of it using either the \(c\)-gram model or the language-overapproximating WFDFA, then pass the top candidates to the conditional reranker.
The admissible set remains exact throughout; learning only changes the order in which valid repairs are discovered and displayed.
